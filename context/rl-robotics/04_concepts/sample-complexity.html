Sample complexity

How much interaction it takes to know something: separating two means a distance Δ apart needs Ω(1/Δ2) samples, and on hardware every sample is measured in robot-seconds.

Where it sits

  • Unifying diagram cell: cross-cutting, the currency in which every method in the right-hand column is priced
  • Failure mode addressed: none; it is the budget against which exploration, model learning and offline data are all judged
  • Course first appearance: Week 4

Core idea

Weeks 1 to 3 spent computation and Week 4 starts spending experiments, so the course needs a unit for the second resource. The unit comes from a hypothesis test: two arms whose means differ by Δ are told apart only once their confidence intervals separate, and the width of an interval falls like 1/n​. Small differences are therefore expensive to resolve, and expensive in a way no estimator can negotiate away, because the bound is a statement about the information the samples carry.

Formal definition

For σ-sub-Gaussian rewards, the empirical mean of n samples lies within σ2ln(1/δ)/n​ of the truth with probability 1−δ, so separating two means at confidence 1−δ needs
n ≳ Δ28σ2ln(1/δ)​
samples from each. Priced in Regret, establishing that arm i is suboptimal costs about 1/Δi2​ pulls at Δi​ each, so the unavoidable bill for learning that the arm is bad is 1/Δi​. The same quantity appears inside UCB1’s count bound ni​≤8lnT/Δi2​, with ln(1/δ) replaced by lnT because the confidence must hold at every round, and inside the Lai-Robbins coefficient once the relative entropy is expanded for small gaps.

Connection to classical control

System identification asks the same question and answers it with the same 1/n​ rate: parameter variance falls with the number of informative samples, and the informativeness is governed by the excitation. What Week 4 adds is the price tag. Persistent excitation asks for enough richness and never asks what the richness costs, whereas a sample-complexity statement converts the requirement into hardware hours and lets it be compared against the value of the resulting controller.

Key limitation

The bound counts samples and says nothing about which samples are available or what collecting them does to the robot. A gap that takes 400 trials to resolve is cheap in simulation and expensive on hardware, and some of those trials may be inadmissible in the sense of Safe exploration. The counting also assumes independent draws from a fixed distribution, an assumption that the state breaks from Week 5 onward, where coverage rather than sample count becomes the binding constraint.

Anchor number for the course

Ten candidate controllers separated by Δ=0.05 on a normalised success metric, evaluated by 30-second trials, need on the order of 10×400×30s≈33 hours of robot time to rank, and 33 hours is a floor: the confidence factor and the reward variance both multiply it, giving several times that for Bernoulli-like outcomes near 50% at δ=0.05. Every later method is measured in the same currency.

Week 5 adds the second entry. Evaluating one fixed swing-up controller on a 961-state discretisation of the Torque-limited pendulum, to an RMS error of 1.62 against an exact Vπ, costs 79350 transitions, which is 2.2 hours of hardware time at 10 Hz. No improvement step appears anywhere in that figure. The two numbers sit on the same axis and should be read together: 33 hours to rank ten controllers you already have, 2.2 hours to learn what one of them is worth from every state.

Week 6 adds the third entry, and it prices a branch of the diagram instead of a task. On the same 961-cell chain with the swing-up controller removed and five admissible torques, Q-learning reaches 97% of J⋆ after 7.5×105 transitions, which is 21 hours at 10 Hz, and Policy gradient (REINFORCE) is still 8.7% short after 6.5×106. That factor of at least 8.6 is a lower bound, since the policy method never reaches the value method’s level inside its budget, and the inter-seed spread at the matched budget is 18.4 against 2.4. The experiment runs on ground chosen to favour tabular value learning and says nothing about continuous action spaces or structured policy classes.
